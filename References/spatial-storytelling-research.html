Research Presentation · June 2026

When place becomes a medium for story

3D scanning is no longer reserved for engineers and surveyors. It is now in the hands of architects, journalists, and community documentarians. This shift creates both an opportunity and an obligation — to build tools that let people record, preserve, and share the stories of places before they disappear.

01 — The Technological Shift

3D capture has moved from lab to pocket

Three technologies have matured simultaneously, making photorealistic 3D capture accessible to anyone with a modern smartphone. This convergence is not incremental — it changes who gets to document the world.

01

Photogrammetry & Mesh

Using overlapping photographs to reconstruct 3D geometry, photogrammetry has been used in engineering and surveying for decades. Apps like Polycam and RealityScan now run this pipeline on a phone, producing exportable mesh files (GLB, OBJ) in minutes — no specialised hardware required.

02

Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)

Introduced in 2023, Gaussian Splatting represents a scene as millions of 3D ellipsoids rather than a mesh. The result is photorealistic and renders in real-time at 1080p. By 2026, apps like Scaniverse generate splats on-device, and formats like .spz allow web delivery. Khronos has formalised a glTF extension, signalling mainstream adoption.

03

Point Cloud & LiDAR

iPhone Pro and iPad Pro carry LiDAR sensors that generate precise point clouds — the engineering-grade cousin of photogrammetry. While less visually dramatic, point clouds carry measurable geometry: the foundation for floor plans, BIM models, and spatial analysis. They also initialise 3DGS pipelines more reliably than photography alone.

"The scan is no longer the deliverable. It is the raw material for a story that someone else needs to tell."

These three capture modes are not competing — they are complementary layers. Mesh provides measurable geometry. Point clouds provide engineering precision. Gaussian Splatting provides photorealistic visual presence. A storyteller today can choose the right layer for the right purpose, or combine all three from a single capture session.

The cost and friction barrier has dropped below the threshold that matters: a motivated individual with a phone can now document a building at a level of spatial fidelity that, five years ago, required a professional survey team.

02 — The Cultural Need

Places disappear. Stories disappear faster.

Urban change — demolition, redevelopment, gentrification — has always outpaced documentation. What is new is that the tools to document now exist at the community level. What is missing is a platform designed around storytellers, not surveyors.

Architects, journalists, oral historians, and residents have always carried knowledge of places that institutional archives never capture. The texture of a building, the way light enters a particular room, the memory of who lived there and what happened — this knowledge is fragile. It exists in people, not in systems, and it disappears when people leave or when buildings are demolished.

The platforms that currently exist for 3D capture — Matterport, Scaniverse, Polycam — are built around the scan as a deliverable. They solve a professional documentation problem. They do not solve a storytelling problem. The story is an afterthought, if it exists at all.

Meanwhile, community-led heritage projects operate in the opposite direction: rich in narrative, thin on spatial capture. Oral history archives, local documentation groups, and independent journalists are recording stories but not places. When the building comes down, the story loses its spatial anchor.

"The gap is not in the technology. It is in the workflow — the missing bridge between spatial capture and community narrative."

A platform designed for community storytellers would treat the 3D scan as shared infrastructure — a location that anyone can use as the setting for their own account. Multiple voices, one place. The same building, documented by an architect, remembered by a resident, and reported on by a journalist, could carry all three stories simultaneously without one overwriting the other.

03 — UX Research

Two questions that shape the platform design

Before evaluating tools or writing code, two structural questions need answers. They determine not just the user interface, but the underlying data model and the kind of authorship the platform enables.

Question 1 — What is the root browsing layer?

Most platforms default to a map as the primary entry point. A map is intuitive for spatial data. But a map organises by location, not by intent. It answers "what is here?" — not "what is this story about?" or "who told it?".

For a community storytelling platform, story is the more meaningful primary unit. A story may span multiple locations. A location may hold multiple stories by different authors. The map becomes a discovery tool — a secondary browse mode for the question "what stories exist near here?" — rather than the primary interface.

Question 2 — Where does the narrative live relative to the scan?

This question produces two distinct modes of spatial storytelling, each valid, each suited to different kinds of stories and different kinds of storytellers.

Mode A

Narrative inside the scan

The story is experienced within the 3D space. The viewer flies through the scan; hotspots, audio, and text appear at specific spatial coordinates. The scan is the stage. Authorship requires placing waypoints and annotating them with media.

  • Best for immersive, place-led storytelling
  • Interior spaces, rooms, architectural detail
  • Oral narrative tied to specific locations in the scan
  • Museum-style or journalistic presentation
Current tool
StorySplat — no-code, exports HTML
Mode B

Narrative around the scan

The story is a page — text, images, audio, video — and the 3D scan is one rich media element embedded within it. The viewer reads and listens, and can step into the scan at relevant moments. The narrative controls the pacing.

  • Best for long-form narrative journalism
  • Stories that span multiple locations
  • Authors who write first, scan second
  • Audiences who prefer reading to navigating
Current tool combination
Splat Labs embed + ArcGIS StoryMaps

Both modes use the same underlying scan asset. The scan is shared infrastructure; the story is the act of authorship. A single location can have multiple stories in either mode, by different contributors — without any one story overwriting another.

Proposed workflow — using existing tools
Step 01

Capture

Scaniverse / 3D Scanner App on iPhone. Export .ply or .splat for 3DGS work; GLB or OBJ for mesh documentation.

Step 02

Asset storage

Single source of truth. Supabase Storage or Cloudflare R2. Metadata (GPS, name, status, contributor, license) in PostGIS database.

Step 03

Story authoring

Mode A: StorySplat for narrative inside the scan. Mode B: Splat Labs embed + ArcGIS StoryMaps for narrative around the scan.

Step 04

Discovery layer

A lightweight index — initially a Notion database or Airtable — listing all stories by location, district, contributor, and mode.

Mode A — Inside the scan

StorySplat: waypoints, hotspots, spatial audio. Exports as standalone HTML. Embeddable anywhere. Suited to immersive, place-led narrative.

Mode B — Around the scan

Long-form page: text, images, audio embed. 3D scan iframed via Splat Labs. Suited to journalism, oral history, multi-location stories.

05 — Platform Vision

A platform built for community storytellers

The research points toward a platform that does not yet exist: one where the story is the primary unit, the scan is shared infrastructure, and communities — not institutions — hold authority over their own spatial heritage.

AI-assisted development tools now make this buildable at a fraction of the effort that would have been required two years ago. The question is no longer "can we build it?" but "what should it do first, and for whom?" — questions that only real use can answer.

📍

Story as primary unit

Not a map of scans, but a collection of stories. Each story has one author, one intent, and one or more locations as its scenes. The map is a browse mode, not the entry point.

🔁

Shared scan infrastructure

A location's 3D scan is captured once and reused freely. Multiple contributors can tell different stories about the same place without duplicating the spatial data.

🔓

Open formats, portable data

No lock-in. Stories and scans export in open formats (glTF, OGG, HTML). Contributors own what they upload. The platform does not survive on vendor dependency.

🎙

Multiple voices, one place

A building holds different truths for different people. The platform holds all of them simultaneously — the architect, the resident, the journalist — without one overwriting another.

⚖️

Community authority

Granular access control. Right to withdraw. Sensitive content handling. Licensing per item. The CARE Principles — Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics — as a governance frame, not an afterthought.

📱

Browser-first, mobile-ready

Most contributors and viewers will use a phone. No app installs, no headset required. VR and WebXR as enhancement, not prerequisite.

06 — Next Steps

From research to first proof of concept

The workflow is designed to validate assumptions before any custom development begins. Each step produces evidence that informs what to build, and in what order.

1

Select one location, build one story twice

Take a scan that already exists. Build the same story in Mode A (StorySplat) and Mode B (Splat Labs + Notion or StoryMaps). Show both versions to people outside the project and observe how they navigate — not what they say, but what they do.

Validate
2

Test the authoring experience with non-technical contributors

The hardest single feature to build well is the hotspot authoring tool. Before specifying it, watch someone who is not a developer attempt to place hotspots and link media in StorySplat. The friction they encounter is the product brief.

Discover
3

Stress-test mobile performance

Load a splat file on an iPhone 12 and a mid-range Android phone over a 4G connection. File size, load time, and frame rate determine what is actually deliverable to a community audience — not what is theoretically possible on a desktop.

Technical
4

Design the data model before the interface

Story → Scene → Location → Scan is the proposed hierarchy. Before wireframing any UI, validate this model against three or four real stories that contributors want to tell. If the model breaks, the UI will too.

Architecture
5

Build the minimum platform with AI-assisted development

Once the data model is validated and the key UX decisions are made, a first version of the platform — map browse, story pages, scan embed, contributor upload — is buildable rapidly with current AI development tools. The research phase exists precisely to make this build purposeful rather than exploratory.
